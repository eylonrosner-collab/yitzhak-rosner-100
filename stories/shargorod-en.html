May 2011

Shargorod (English)

מאת יצחק (ז׳אק) רוזנר

Shargorod: (Written in May 2011 by Yitzkhak Rosner) On October 9th 1941, my family together with all the Jews of Gura Humorului were deported to Transnistria - a region across the river Dnieste that has just been captured by the German and the Romanian armies, and according to the Führer's decision was delivered to the control and management of the fascist government of Marshal Antonescu. We stayed there in a place called Shargorod until the end of May 1944, when the area was liberated by the Red Army. This was a very important time in my life. Here is the story: In the last year before we were deported to Transnitria, we lived in Gura Humorului, a small town located about 50 km southeast of Moldovitsa, the village where my parents lived in during my childhood years. In the summer of 1940 - after the fascist regime of Antonescu strengthened and the influence of Nazi Germany on Romanian government policy was already a fact - Romanian authorities began to enact laws similar to the German race laws (the Nuremberg Laws). Among other things, they passed a law according to which Jews were forbidden from living in villages, under the pretext that Jews are exploiting and robbing the rural population. We were given three days to leave the house and move to an urban place of living. Since my mother had relatives in Gura - the Ellenbogens – it was decided we move there, hoping that the "bad times" shall soon pass and we’ll be able to return to our homes in Moldovitsa. We gathered some basic belongings, locked the house and bolted it in the presence of two officers of the Legionnaire police, mounted the train and arrived in Gura to an apartment my parents had rented in advance. We lived there about a year – a strange one I should say – in Gura Humorului, until our deportation to Transnistria. During this period, more anti-Jewish laws were enacted: Jewish students were expelled from all schools, Jewish doctors were allowed to treat only Jewish patients, all Jewish government officials were dismissed, etc. At the same time, the Germans have completely took over the Romanian economy. All exports of raw materials - oil, wheat, wood - that had until then went mainly to Britain and France, was now exported to Germany. In this context, my father's company was also forced to export its product, timber - which has until then been mainly exported to Britain and also partly to Palestine - to Germany. But this created a problem: after the expulsion from Moldovitsa and the prohibition for Jews to live and work in villages, there was no one to manage my father’s factory and he was not able to fulfill his export quotas. Despite pressure from various entities related to export on my father to go to Moldovitsa and ensure shipments got out on time, he refused, fearing the Legionnaire Romanian police (armed groups of the extreme Romanian fascist youth, which operated in parallel to the official authorities). Then, under pressure from these entities, my father was given a personal bodyguard whose job was to ensure his safety during his travels to Moldovitsa and his work there. The bodyguard was no other than a German corporal allocated by the Wehrmacht to protect the jew Littman Rosner against Romanian fascists. Every Monday morning the corporal presented himself at our home, accompanying my father on his trip to Moldovitsa and his work there, lived with my father at our home [in Moldovitza] and return him to his family in Gura on the weekend. The relationship between my father and the corporal were very correct and there were cases where my mother had invited the corporal to Sunday lunch. Imagine the scene: We, four Jews under the fascist regime, with a Star of David on our lapel and a constant fear of the police and the Romanian authorities, sit around the table with a German soldier with his rifle lying on the couch, eating and talking in a friendly, civilized manner, like the real world outside does not exist. This idyll ended finally in October 1941. The rumors started about a month before. The rumors industry was very active at that time. There was no TV or Internet and radios were confiscated from the Jews. So the Jews invented the news agency YPA - Yiddish Plotkes Agentur (יידישע פלוטקס אגענטור) - which roughly means "Jewish tall tales agency". every day various rumors were spread and every Jew would add to the story and embellish it with whatever came to his mind. But in many cases there was a grain of truth to the YPA news. And so the Jews began to learn that the government has decided to transfer us to the newly conquered area from the Soviets in Ukraine and each family will get a farm and live there like in paradise. On Wednesday, 8 October 1941, the military governor has issued a decree commanding all Jews to leave their homes and report the following day, October 9 at 10:00AM at the train station - each person with a single package he can carry by himself. My father was in Moldovitza and my mother has managed, with the aid of the postmaster Mr Grossu who was a friend of the family, to send the news to my father. And indeed late at night my father arrived, accompanied by the German soldier. After a scene of farewell and best wishes, the German soldier left, never to be seen again. All night long we were packing things we considered necessary and wanted to take with us - clothes, socks, shoes, a minimum of housewares and the like. It was decided that we the children – Nellie and me – will carry a backpack while the parents will each carry a package - a blanket which had been packed with all sorts of things, wrapped with ropes for carrying on the back. The deportation order included a clause forbidding Jews to bring money with them. All cash was supposed to be left at home. Because for months there were rumors about deportation, Dad had prepared a quite substantial amount of money in case of emergency. According to my recollection, the amount was 220,000 Lei - a very respectable sum in those days (about $10,000 of 1941 which today is worth over $100,000). After much discussion and deliberation, it was decided early in the morning that we do not leave our money as ordered by the authorities. My mother undid the lining of her winter coat and sewed a bag with the bills. In the morning when we left to the train station she wore this coat. With all the risks involved, my parents brought the money with us. To those who read this lines, I remind that in those days money was something you hold in your hand. No one was using checks, and credit cards did not exist at all. Later I will tell you the fate of this money. At around 9 am we left home and with the "Pekalach" on the our backs began our path of misery which at the end of two weeks brought us to Shargorod. When we arrived at the square in front of the railway station, dozens of families were already there. The army and the gendarmerie have formed a ring, which once you entered you could not leave. We stood there for a few hours when we were told that we are waiting for a train to take us to our new place of residence. The atmosphere was somber but everybody was hoping that after this trip we would get to a place, where according to the YPA news we could live in peace through the war. We spent the time arranging the sitting on the train: who will sit near the window, how we arrange the packages etc. After hours of waiting, a Romanian officer came out of the station with news that the train will arrive within minutes and that we must maintain the order getting on it... ….
